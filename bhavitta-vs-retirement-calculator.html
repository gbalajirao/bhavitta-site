Bhavitta vs a retirement calculator

A retirement calculator takes one return, applies it every year and tells you whether the money lasts. That is a useful first look, and on a real plan it is usually too optimistic. Here is the same plan run both ways, with what simulation adds and where a calculator is still the right tool.

Short answer. A calculator assumes the same return every year, so it only tells you whether the average lasts. On the same plan (a couple stopping at 45 with ₹3 crore, 9.5% a year, Bangalore inflation, to 90) a constant-return calculation approves up to ₹1.13 lakh a month. Run through 10,000 simulated market paths with the same 9.5% average, that spending lasts in only 35% of them. Spending that lasts in 80% is about ₹65,000 a month, 42% less. Bhavitta also models the plan as life stages (cities, a home, college, a pension, a move) rather than one set of numbers.

What a retirement calculator does

Most online retirement calculators ask for your age, savings, monthly spending, an expected return and an inflation rate. They grow the savings at that return, take out the spending each year, and report whether the money reaches your chosen age, or how much you would need for it to. That is quick, free, needs nothing installed, and is a perfectly good way to get the order of magnitude.

The weakness is the one assumption every year shares: the return. Real markets do not deliver 9.5% each year; they deliver +30% one year and −20% another, and the order matters. Bad years early in retirement, while you are withdrawing, do far more damage than the same bad years later. A constant return cannot show that, so a calculator effectively answers "does the plan work if markets are perfectly average every single year?"

What a single number misses

  • Sequence of returns. The order of good and bad years, which matters most in the first decade of withdrawals. Bhavitta runs either every window of 1928–2025 market history or 10,000 Monte Carlo paths, and its crash-in-year-one guide shows how much a bad start costs.
  • "Lasts every year", not "lasts on average". Bhavitta's survival rate counts a path only if the balance stays above zero in every year to the end of the plan.
  • A life, not one set of numbers. A calculator has one spending figure and one inflation rate. A Bhavitta plan is a timeline of stages: working in one city, retiring to another, a home with its mortgage and sale, a child's college years, a pension or Social Security from a later age, EPF/PPF/NPS paying out at their own ages, health insurance rising faster than inflation, one-time costs such as a wedding.
  • Which assumption matters. The stress test re-runs the plan across a five-by-five grid of return and inflation, so you can see whether your plan is fragile to markets or to prices.
  • What one change does. Sliders and what-if chips (a job loss, a crash, retiring two years earlier, a move abroad) re-run the whole simulation and show the before and after, and Compare puts up to three scenarios side by side with a one-line verdict.

When a calculator is the right tool

A calculator is faster when you want a rough target in thirty seconds, are on a computer, or are comparing a handful of round numbers. And Bhavitta has real limits that are worth knowing before you install it:

  • It is not a tax calculator: one blended rate per stage (estimated from the Indian slabs for an Indian city, if you ask), no US brackets, filing status or treaty rules.
  • It has one portfolio return, with no asset allocation, bond ladder or glide path, and no 401(k)/IRA withdrawal rules or required distributions.
  • Historical replay uses US equity history for every plan; for rupee savings, use the Indian Monte Carlo presets instead.
  • There is no web version yet, only iPhone and Android.

Common questions

Is a retirement calculator accurate?

It is accurate about one scenario: markets delivering the same return every year. Real returns vary, and bad years early in retirement do lasting damage, so spending set at a constant-return calculator's limit lasted in only about a third of simulated market paths in Bhavitta's engine.

What is the difference between a retirement calculator and Monte Carlo simulation?

A calculator applies one return every year and gives one answer. Monte Carlo runs the plan thousands of times with returns that vary year to year around the same average, and reports the share of runs in which the money lasted. Bhavitta runs 10,000 by default, or replays every window of 1928–2025 market history.

How much less should I spend than a calculator says?

In Bhavitta's engine, for a couple retiring in India on ₹3–5 crore, spending that lasts in 80% of simulated paths was 35–42% below what a constant 9.5% return supports. Your own figure depends on your plan, which is the reason to simulate it rather than apply a rule.

What is the best retirement planning app in India?

It depends on what you need. For a quick corpus figure, a free online calculator is enough. If you want to test a plan with stages (cities, a home, children, EPF/PPF/NPS, a move between India and abroad) against market history and see how likely it is to last, Bhavitta does that for free, on your phone, without an account.

Does Bhavitta replace a financial adviser?

No. It is an educational planning tool for testing whether a plan holds, not advice. For your own situation, talk to a SEBI-registered adviser in India or a fiduciary adviser in the US.
